Read-to-Unlock Quest

A quest where you have to actually read the project document before you can post a review. People who share answers get caught automatically, and every approved review can be checked by anyone.

How it works

This platform sells trusted reviews to projects. If farmers write the reviews, it's not just wasted reward money, the whole product becomes useless.

So I built the quest around one rule: the only way through is to actually read the document.

The 3 steps

Step 1Easy tasks

Follow, join, share. These are checked automatically but they're easy to fake, so they only give 5 XP each, max 15.

Step 2Read and pass the quiz

5 questions, you need 4 right. You get one try. Passing opens step 3.

Step 3Submit one review

One review per person. Someone from the team reads it and scores it from 1 to 5. 3 or more gets approved.

How the quiz catches farmers

Everyone gets one of 5 versions of the project document. All versions are the same, only a few numbers are different.

The quiz has 5 questions. 3 are the same for everyone. The other 2 ask about the numbers that change. I call these anchor questions. So the right answer depends on which version you got.

What the person didScoreWhat happens
Read their own version4 or 5 of 5Pass, step 3 opens
Copied answers from someone with a different version3 of 5Fail, marked as shared answers
Just made a mistake3 of 5Fail, can try again tomorrow with new questions

The good part is that the system can tell the last two apart by itself. If your wrong answer is the right answer for another version, you copied it. If it doesn't match any version, it was just a mistake. Nobody has to check it by hand.

I also shuffle the order of the questions and options for every person. On its own this doesn't stop much, because people can still share the answer text. But it pushes them to share the actual answers, and that's exactly what the anchor questions catch.

Two checks for two kinds of cheating

Anchor check, at the quiz

Catches people who share quiz answers before they even write a review, so it doesn't waste the reviewer's time. It's the only check that catches someone who writes their own review but got the quiz answers from someone else.

Version check, at the review

Catches a review copied from someone who had a different version, because the numbers in it won't match. It's the only check that catches someone who read properly but then copied another person's review.

What I found

If I turn off either check, the approval rate actually goes up, and a farmer gets paid. That's because farmed reviews look good, so the reviewer approves them. So approval rate alone can't tell you if you're being farmed.

Why on-chain

Most of this quest could work on a normal web2 site. This is the part that couldn't.

The problem with a normal review site

When a project pays for reviews on a normal site, readers have to trust the platform on 3 things: the document wasn't changed after people reviewed it, reviewers saw the version the platform says they saw, and the dates are real. You just have to take their word for it.

What goes on-chain (testnet only)

1. Fingerprint of all 5 versions

Before the campaign starts, the platform posts a hash (a fingerprint) of each version. The hash doesn't show what's inside, but later anyone can check the document wasn't changed.

2. Signed review (optional)

If someone has a wallet, they can sign their review together with their version number. Then anyone can check what they wrote, which version it was about, and when.

3. Nothing else

Review scores stay off-chain because they're a human decision, and a mistake shouldn't be permanent. Who got which version also stays private until the campaign ends, otherwise it becomes the answer key.

Why the fingerprint matters

Without it, if I tell someone "you used the wrong version", it's just my word against theirs. With the hash posted on-chain, anyone can check who's right. That matters when someone is losing a reward over it.

What it's not

It's not proof that someone owns tokens, it's not proof of reserves, and it's not saying the project is a good investment. It only shows that a person read a document and wrote about it. No buying, no mainnet, no real rewards.

Wallet is optional

Signing gives 10 XP and a "verified" badge, but it's never required. Someone with no wallet can still top the XP table, and their review counts exactly the same. In the test, .

The business side

What a project pays for is 25 real reviews. Here's what it takes to deliver them.

My assumptions, not real data: a reviewer costs 12 USD an hour and takes 5 minutes per review. The ratio is what matters, not the exact amounts.

Simple way to read it: when the checks are off, farmers write good-looking reviews and they get approved. More than half of the approved reviews are fake, so the reviewer has to read more than double to get 25 real ones, and farmers get paid on top of that.

With the checks on, a real review costs about 55% less and no farmer gets paid.

When to stop

  • Nothing kicks in below 50 sign-ups a week.
  • Shared answer or copied review rate over 10% in a week: pause and post new versions, because answers are going around.
  • More reviews waiting than the reviewer can do in 2 weeks: stop inviting people.
  • Approval rate under 70% for 2 weeks: make the quiz harder.
  • Cost per real review over 2 USD: stop and find which check is leaking.

PRD

Problem

This platform sells trusted reviews. Quests pay for easy tasks that are simple to fake. The one task that actually matters, a real review, needs a person to read it. And farmers using AI can write reviews good enough to get approved. So farmed reviews waste reward money and also make the product useless.

Goal

Deliver reviews a project can point to and anyone can verify, at a cost one reviewer can keep up with.

Who it's for

  • People taking part, with or without a wallet
  • The reviewer, who has around 3 to 4 hours a week
  • Projects paying for reviews
  • The growth team, who decide how many people to invite

What to build, in order

1. Duplicate check, versions and the on-chain fingerprint. Nothing else can be trusted without this.

  • If the device, email or KYC matches another account, it gets 0 XP and can't submit.
  • Each person gets a random version when they sign up. It's saved on the server and never shown in the page, the link or on-chain.
  • The hashes of all 5 versions are posted on testnet before the campaign opens.

2. Quiz with anchor questions and one try.

  • 3 normal questions and 2 anchor questions, marked against the person's own version.
  • If a wrong anchor answer matches another version, it's logged as shared answers, not a mistake.
  • One try. If you fail, you wait 24 hours and get different questions.
  • Questions and options are shuffled for every person.
  • The review step is blocked on the server until the quiz is passed, not just hidden on screen.

3. Version check on reviews.

  • Numbers in a review are matched against the person's version.
  • A number from another version: rejected, with the reason shown and a way to appeal that can be checked against the hash.
  • A review with no numbers is never penalised.
  • Sent in under 30 seconds: held for someone to look at, not banned.

4. Review queue and cost dashboard.

  • Oldest first, one at a time, time spent is recorded.
  • A scoring guide with an example of a 2 and a 4, so reviewers score the same way.
  • The dashboard shows how many are waiting, approval rate, shared answer rate and cost per real review.

5. Optional signing and badges.

  • No level and no approval needs a wallet. Someone with no wallet can top the table.
  • If signing fails, the person only loses those 10 XP and can try again.
  • An approved and signed review gets a "verified" badge once the campaign ends and the versions are revealed.

Channel test

Where: a Telegram announcement with two links, people split at random. Not actually run.

  • A, reward first: "Complete quests and climb the leaderboard."
  • B, reading first: "Read the document, pass the quiz, and the team will read your review."

What I expect: B gets fewer sign-ups, but more approved reviews per sign-up and a lower cost per real review.

How I'd decide: go with B if its main number is at least 5 points higher and its cost per real review isn't worse. Don't decide on approval rate alone.

Size: 200 people per link, up to 2 weeks. That's my guess, not a proper calculation. It's also more than one reviewer can handle, so the test needs a second reviewer or fewer people.

Weekly update, week 1

Numbers

  • 13 signed up, 12 unique, 5 reached the reviewer, 4 approved
  • Approval rate 80%, shared answer rate 17%, about 1.25 USD per real review
  • 2 of the 4 approved reviews came from people with no wallet
  • 1 of the 4 approved reviews is signed on testnet

What happened

Q09 and Q10 both answered the anchor questions with version A's numbers, but Q09 had version B and Q10 had version D. So they shared answers. Both were stopped at the quiz, before they took any of the reviewer's time. Q11 read their own version properly but then copied someone else's review, and the version check caught that.

Q07 also got 3 of 5, but their wrong answers didn't match any version. So it was just a mistake. They keep their XP and can try again tomorrow. Same score as the farmers, completely different result, and the system decided it by itself.

If you turn off either check in Try it, the approval rate goes up while a farmer gets paid, because farmed reviews look good. That's why approval rate shows health, not honesty.

Decisions

  • Shared answer rate is 17%, over the 10% line. Post a new set of 5 versions before the next batch of invites.
  • One reviewer doing 40 reviews a week gets about 32 approved, which covers roughly 190 sign-ups a week. Keep invites at that level until there's a second reviewer.
  • Only 25% of approved reviews are signed. Worth testing if showing the badge earlier gets more people to sign, since signed reviews are the ones a project can really point to.

Ways people could still cheat

  • Someone with 5 accounts can read all 5 versions. This stops mass farming, not one determined person.
  • Using different devices and emails can get around the duplicate check.
  • A signature proves when and what someone wrote, not who they are. A farmer can sign too.
  • Two reviewers might score the same review differently.
  • A typo in one version would reject honest people and look just like farming.
