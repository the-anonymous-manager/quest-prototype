Read-to-Unlock Quest

A quest where you have to read the project disclosure before you can submit a review, farmers who share answers are caught automatically, and every approved review can be verified by anyone against the exact document version it was written about.

How it works

The platform sells credible review coverage. A farmed review isn't just a wasted reward, it's a defective product.

So the quest is built so that reading is the only way through, and so the result can be checked by someone who doesn't trust the platform.

Three steps

Step 1Easy tasks

Follow, join, share. Checked automatically. 5 XP each, capped at 15, because they're easy to fake.

Step 2Read and pass the quiz

5 questions, need 4 right, one attempt. Passing unlocks step 3.

Step 3One submission

One question or review per person. A person reads it and scores it 1 to 5. 3 or above is approved.

The quiz: 3 general questions and 2 anchor questions

Everyone gets one of five versions of the disclosure. The versions are identical except for a few numbers. Three quiz questions are the same for everyone. Two are anchor questions, and their correct answers are the numbers that differ between versions.

What happensScoreResult
Read your own version properly4 or 5 of 5Pass, step 3 unlocks
Used someone else's answers from a different version3 of 5Fail, and flagged as a shared answer key
Honest mistake, answers match no version3 of 5Fail, retry tomorrow with new questions

The system can tell those last two apart by itself. If a wrong anchor answer is the correct answer for a different version, that's a shared key. If it matches no version, it's a mistake. Same score, completely different meaning, no human needed.

Question and answer order is shuffled per person too. On its own that's only a speed bump, since people can share answer text instead of positions. What it does is force farmers to share the actual answers, which is exactly what the anchor questions catch.

Two checks, two different attacks

Anchor check, at the quiz

Catches shared quiz answers before anyone writes anything, so it costs no review time. It's the only check that catches a farmer who writes their own text and quotes no numbers.

Version check, at the submission

Catches a submission copied from an account on a different version. It's the only check that catches someone who read their own version honestly and then copied somebody's review.

The main finding

Turn off either check and the approval rate goes up while a farmer gets paid. The farmers write good reviews, so the reviewer approves them. Approval rate can't tell you whether you're being farmed.

Why this is on-chain, and what it earns

Most of this quest would work on any web2 review site. Here is the part that would not.

The problem with a web2 review site

A project pays for a review round. The reviews appear. But a reader has to take the platform's word for three things: that the disclosure wasn't quietly edited after people reviewed it, that reviewers saw the version being claimed, and that the timestamps are real. Every one of those is just trust in the platform.

What goes on-chain, on testnet

1. Version commitment

Before the campaign opens, the platform publishes a hash of each of the five disclosure versions. The hashes reveal nothing, but afterwards anyone can check that the version someone was shown existed before the campaign started and wasn't edited later.

2. Review attestation

Optional. A participant signs a hash of their submission plus their version id. Their review becomes independently verifiable: this text, about this version, at this time, before the project replied.

3. Nothing else

Review scores stay off-chain, because they're a human judgment and a mistake shouldn't be permanent. Which version someone got stays private until the campaign closes, or it becomes the answer key.

Why the version commitment matters more than it looks

Without it, "you quoted the wrong version" is the platform's word against the participant's. With it, an appeal can be settled by anyone with the hash. The anti-farming system becomes auditable instead of something users have to trust, which matters when the penalty is losing a reward.

What it is not

Not proof of holdings, not verified reserves, not a signal that anything is worth buying. An attestation says a person read a document and wrote about it. It does not say they own anything, and it says nothing about whether the project is a good investment. No purchase, no mainnet transaction, no real reward anywhere in this.

The wallet stays optional, and the numbers show it

An attestation earns 10 XP and a "verified against version X" badge. It is never required. A participant with no wallet can reach the top of the XP table and their review is approved and counted exactly the same, it just doesn't carry the badge. In the simulation, .

The business case

The platform's product is coverage a project can point to. That's worth more when a third party can verify it, and worth nothing when it's farmed. So the checks aren't housekeeping, they're what protects the thing being sold.

Assumptions, not measured: a review round is 25 approved reviews, a reviewer costs 12 USD an hour and takes 5 minutes per review. The ratios matter here, not the absolute numbers.

With the checks on, a credible review costs about 55% less, and one reviewer delivers roughly twice as many. That's the argument for spending engineering time on this, in terms a founder can act on.

Stop rules

  • Nothing triggers below 50 sign-ups in a week.
  • Shared key rate or copied review rate above 10% in a week: pause and publish a new set of five versions, because an answer key is going around.
  • Review queue bigger than two weeks of reviewer capacity: stop sending invites.
  • Approval rate below 70% for two weeks: make the quiz harder.
  • Cost per credible review above 2 USD: stop and find out which check is leaking.

Synthetic dataset

All 13 people are made up. Each one tests a specific case.

PRD

Problem

The platform sells credible review coverage. Quest campaigns pay for cheap tasks that are easy to fake, the one task that matters needs a person to read it, and a farmer using AI writes reviews good enough to be approved. Farmed reviews don't just waste the reward budget, they are a defective product.

Goal

Deliver review rounds a project can point to and a reader can verify, at a cost per credible review one reviewer can sustain.

Users

  • Participants, with or without a wallet
  • The reviewer, who has about 3 to 4 hours a week
  • Projects paying for a review round
  • The growth lead, who decides how many people to invite

Backlog, in priority order

1. Duplicate check, version assignment and version commitment. Nothing else is trustworthy without it.

  • Same device, email or KYC hash as an existing account: earns 0 XP, can't submit.
  • Each person gets a random version at sign-up, stored on the server, never exposed in the page, URL or on-chain.
  • The five version hashes are published on testnet before the campaign opens, so the assignment can be audited later.

2. Quiz with anchor questions and one attempt.

  • 3 general plus 2 anchor questions, marked against the person's own version.
  • A wrong anchor answer that matches another version's correct answer is logged as a shared key, not a mistake.
  • One attempt. A fail starts a 24 hour wait and the retry uses different questions.
  • Question and option order shuffled per person.
  • Submission blocked on the server until the quiz is passed, not just hidden in the interface.

3. Version check on submissions.

  • Numbers quoted in a submission are matched against the person's version.
  • A number from another version: rejected, with the reason and an appeal route that can be settled against the published hash.
  • A submission quoting no numbers is never penalised.
  • Submitted in under 30 seconds: held for a person to look at, not banned.

4. Review queue and the cost dashboard.

  • Oldest first, one at a time, minutes spent recorded.
  • Scoring guide with an example of a 2 and a 4 so reviewers agree.
  • Dashboard shows queue size, approval rate, shared key rate and cost per credible review.

5. Optional attestation and badges.

  • No level and no approval needs a wallet. A wallet decliner can top the table.
  • A failed signature only loses its own 10 XP and can be retried.
  • An approved review with an attestation shows a "verified against version X" badge once the campaign closes and versions are revealed.

Channel experiment

Channel: Telegram announcement, two links split at random. Not run.

  • A, reward-led: "Complete quests and climb the leaderboard."
  • B, reading-led: "Read the disclosure, pass the quiz, and the team reads your review."

Expected result: B gets fewer sign-ups but more approved reviews per sign-up and a lower cost per credible review.

Decision rule: pick B if its main metric is at least 5 points higher and its cost per credible review is no worse. Don't decide on approval rate alone.

Size: 200 eligible people per link, up to 2 weeks. This is an assumption, not a calculation. It's also more than one reviewer can handle, so the test needs a second reviewer or fewer people.

Weekly decision memo, week 1

Numbers

  • 13 signed up, 12 unique, 5 reached the reviewer, 4 approved
  • Approval rate 80%, shared key rate 17%, cost per credible review about 1.25 USD
  • 2 of 4 approved reviews came from people who never connected a wallet
  • 1 of 4 approved reviews carries a testnet attestation

What happened

Q09 and Q10 both answered the anchor questions with version A's numbers. Q09 was on version B and Q10 on version D, so they were sharing answers. They were stopped at the quiz, before either of them cost the reviewer a minute. Q11 read their own version honestly and then copied someone else's review text, which the version check caught at submission.

Q07 also failed with 3 of 5, but their wrong anchor answers matched no version at all. That's an honest mistake, so they keep their XP and can retry tomorrow with different questions. Same score as the farmers, opposite treatment, decided automatically.

Turn off either check in the prototype and the approval rate goes up while a farmer gets paid, because farmed reviews read well. That's why approval rate is a health metric and not an integrity metric.

Decisions

  • Shared key rate is 17%, above the 10% line. Publish a fresh set of five version hashes before the next invite batch.
  • One reviewer at 40 reviews a week delivers about 32 approved reviews, so roughly 190 sign-ups a week. Cap invites there until a second reviewer is added.
  • Attested share is low at 25%. Worth testing whether showing the badge earlier in the flow lifts it, since attested reviews are the ones a project can actually point to.

Abuse risks

  • Someone running 5 accounts can read all 5 versions. This stops mass farming, not a determined small farmer.
  • Multiple devices and emails can beat the duplicate check.
  • An attestation proves timing and content, not identity. A farmer can attest too.
  • Two reviewers may score the same review differently.
  • A typo in one version would reject honest people and look exactly like farming.

Next validation step

Take 20 real submissions from a past campaign, with permission, and have two people score them separately. That checks whether the scoring guide works and how long a review really takes. Every capacity and cost number assumes 5 minutes.
